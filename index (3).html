Is It Possible to Leave the Healthcare System to the Private Sector?

By Ayberk Ceyran. Written July 2026, revised November 2026.

Download the PDFTürkçe oku

Introduction

Whether the healthcare system can be left to the private sector is a debate with not only economic but also ethical, legal, and social dimensions. Neoliberal and anarcho-capitalist schools of thought tend to favor private-sector-heavy models, while social democratic, social liberal, and various left-wing views argue that the public sector must play a strong role. For this reason, evaluating this issue raises a humanitarian question that goes beyond the debate over economic efficiency.

Looking at specific countries, Cuba is often cited as one of the countries where healthcare is most accessible: it has the highest number of doctors per person in the world [1]. However, due to the embargoes currently in place and the country’s economic crisis, access to advanced medical technology is limited, and medicines are often in short supply [2]. In the exact opposite case, in the United States, the fact that the healthcare system is largely private pushes technology and efficiency to their maximum levels, while creating a huge gap in access to healthcare between the wealthy and everyone else.

The United States: What Happens When the Market Decides?

So let’s get to our main question: can the healthcare system really be left entirely to the private sector? For instance, in the United States, drug companies have long been free to set the prices of cancer drugs; only since the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 has Medicare, the public health program for older Americans, started to negotiate the prices of some of them [3]. This brings to mind a question attributed to former Turkish Prime Minister Süleyman Demirel, one I’m also very fond of: “Does an industry that sells medicine actually want health?” It might sound to some like an offhand remark, but there is a very deep truth behind it.

The United States is not entirely private either: in 2024, federal, state and local governments together paid for almost half of all health spending [4]. Still, in a largely private system like this, large organizations such as the American Cancer Society, whose total assets are about 1.8 billion dollars [5], and the H. Lee Moffitt Cancer Center support people who would otherwise struggle to access care. Yet even if we assume there are no victims who go unheard, what happens if, during an economic crisis, the donors behind these organizations reduce or completely stop their donations? This is not without historical precedent. During the Great Depression that began in 1929, for example, donations to US charitable hospitals fell to about 40 percent of their earlier level, and nearly 400 nonprofit hospitals closed within five years [6]. Many people were left to their fate because they could not afford treatment [7]. In other words, the most vulnerable segment of society became an indirect victim of nothing more than a stock market crash or an economic downturn.

This raises the question of just how right it is to leave the system entirely free with no state intervention at all. It becomes even more telling when you consider that the US government requested a record $1.5 trillion military budget for fiscal year 2027 [8]. Given the consequences of leaving the healthcare system entirely to the private sector, one can genuinely find oneself thinking, “Let’s leave healthcare entirely to the state instead.”

Taiwan: A Hybrid Model

It is fair to say that hybrid models have proven more successful worldwide than either fully private or fully state-run systems — at least, satisfaction surveys on healthcare systems across countries point in this direction. An important example is Taiwan, which has ranked first in Numbeo’s Health Care Index, a survey-based ranking of healthcare systems, for eight years in a row [9]. And yet, until 1995, about 41% of Taiwan’s population had no health coverage at all [10]. Afterward, a national health insurance system called National Health Insurance (NHI) was established, and thanks to this hybrid model, the country reached its current level of success in a relatively short period of time.

Digital Health Records: Taiwan and Türkiye

The digital capacity of Taiwan’s system closely resembles the E-Nabız system we have here in Türkiye [11]. Every citizen, as well as every foreign resident, has a chip-embedded NHI Health Card. Through this card, a doctor can access a cloud-based system called MediCloud and see, on a single screen, which hospital took which X-ray of yours in the past, which medications you’ve used, and your allergies [12]. A similar system exists in Türkiye as well — but unlike ours, Taiwan’s system is designed to prevent the same prescriptions and tests from being repeated over and over [12].

Overuse and Administrative Costs in Türkiye

Today, if you go to the emergency room closest to your home, you’ll find a long line and far more people than you’d expect pressuring the doctor for an IV drip just because they have a headache. There are also plenty of people in Türkiye who try to pressure doctors into prescribing medication or ordering unnecessary blood tests. The biggest burden this places on the system is that it clogs up appointments. It’s clear that the E-Nabız system has digital infrastructure just as advanced as Taiwan’s — but one of the main reasons it’s used so heavily is that people abuse it, thinking “It’s free anyway.”

For example, if you book an appointment right now and cancel it three hours before the scheduled time, nobody asks, “Why are you doing this?” As long as you cancelled, there’s no issue. But when doing this repeatedly carries no consequence whatsoever, people stop taking the system seriously. Requesting an IV drip as casually as asking for a bottle of water at a kiosk, and having that request accepted without question, ends up harming the patients who genuinely need care.

On top of making abuses like these impossible, Taiwan’s administrative management cost is only about 1% of spending [13]. In Türkiye, this kind of figure varies from source to source. Studies of individual public hospitals, for example, find that overhead costs — everything other than staff salaries and medicines or medical supplies — make up roughly 16 to 37 percent of a hospital’s total costs [14][15]. These figures are measured differently from Taiwan’s, so they cannot be compared directly, but they suggest that a large share of spending does not go directly to treatment. There can be many reasons for this — one being that hospitals’ arguably unnecessary expenditures and surplus public staff place an additional burden on the system. Whether it’s really necessary, in 2026, for hospitals to employ staff whose only job is to tell people “Second corridor on the right, internal medicine department,” is a separate debate in itself. Instead, digital wayfinding systems (with audio options for people with disabilities), more directional signage, and a simpler hospital layout could largely solve this problem. However, the public-sector hiring that governments carry out around election periods can be seen as a form of populist policy, and this directly reduces the efficiency of state hospitals.

Why Taiwan’s Model Works

The main reason Taiwan’s system developed into one that is both efficient and strong is precisely that it chose a hybrid model free of these problems. You might be surprised, but roughly 80% of hospitals belong to the private sector (83% in 2016) [16]. Yet the state has not left the system largely unregulated, the way the US has. When global pharmaceutical giants or medical equipment manufacturers want to enter the Taiwanese market, they find themselves facing a single counterpart: the Taiwanese state. Acting as the single buyer (monopsony), the state sets the prices it pays for medicines, using prices in other developed countries as a reference, and cuts them when spending goes over its target [17].

The combination of the private sector’s dynamism and efficiency with the state’s regulatory power forms the foundation of the system’s success. While only about 1% of the premiums paid by Taiwan’s roughly 23 million people is spent on administration [13], the remaining resources are channeled to private-sector hospitals and to medicine and medical supply providers. Running all of these processes through a digital, centralized system allows the state to benefit from the private sector’s efficiency without leaving healthcare services at its mercy. Indeed, the success of this model is evident from the fact that it has ranked first in Numbeo’s Health Care Index for eight years [9], alongside public satisfaction of more than 90 percent [10]. Of course, the system is not without problems: critics argue that it keeps costs low partly by overworking healthcare staff [18], and it faces new challenges such as rapid demographic change and rising costs [10]. Compared with the other countries discussed here, however, its overall results remain strong.

Conclusion

In summary, when we think this through with the question “Does an industry that sells medicine actually want health?” at the center, we should recognize health as a fundamental human right and aim to build the most efficient, accessible, and sustainable system possible toward that end. With a structure that preserves the public sector’s oversight role while putting the private sector’s dynamism to work for the system, the hybrid model appears to be one of the strongest options for reaching that goal.
